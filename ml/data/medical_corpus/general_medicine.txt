TITLE: Dengue Fever - Clinical Management Protocol
SOURCE: WHO Dengue Guidelines for Diagnosis, Treatment, Prevention, 2024
CATEGORY: Infectious Disease | Tropical Medicine

Dengue fever is a mosquito-borne viral infection transmitted by Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus mosquitoes. It is caused by four serotypes (DENV-1 to DENV-4). Infection with one serotype provides lifelong immunity to that serotype but only partial temporary immunity to others, with secondary infections carrying higher risk of severe dengue.

Clinical Phases:
1. Febrile Phase (Days 1-7): High fever (40°C/104°F), severe headache, retro-orbital pain, myalgia, arthralgia, rash, mild bleeding (petechiae, gum bleeding)
2. Critical Phase (Days 3-7 of illness): Defervescence period; highest risk for severe dengue with plasma leakage, severe bleeding, organ impairment
3. Recovery Phase: Gradual reabsorption of fluid, improved well-being, return of appetite

CRITICAL MEDICATION WARNING:
⚠️ Do NOT take Aspirin, Ibuprofen, or any NSAID - these increase bleeding risk significantly
✅ SAFE: Paracetamol (Acetaminophen) only - max 4g/day in adults, weight-based dosing in children

Warning Signs of Severe Dengue (Seek IMMEDIATE Emergency Care):
- Severe abdominal pain or persistent vomiting
- Clinical fluid accumulation (ascites, pleural effusion)
- Mucosal bleeding (gums, nose, gums, vaginal)
- Lethargy, restlessness, or irritability
- Liver enlargement > 2 cm
- Laboratory: Rising hematocrit with rapid decrease in platelet count
- Platelet count < 100,000/mm³ with declining trend

Home Care for Non-Severe Dengue:
- Complete bed rest
- Oral rehydration: Drink 2-3 liters of fluids daily (water, ORS, coconut water, fresh juices)
- Tepid sponging for fever (NOT cold water baths)
- Monitor urine output (should be ≥ 0.5 mL/kg/hr)
- Daily blood count monitoring (CBC with platelet count)
- Watch for warning signs especially during Days 3-7

TITLE: Common Upper Respiratory Tract Infections
SOURCE: NICE Clinical Guidelines, 2023; CDC Antibiotic Prescribing Guidelines
CATEGORY: General Medicine | Infectious Disease

Upper Respiratory Tract Infections (URTIs) include the common cold, pharyngitis, sinusitis, and laryngitis. Most are viral in origin (rhinovirus, coronavirus, RSV, influenza, parainfluenza) and are self-limiting.

Common Cold Management:
- Duration: Typically 7-10 days; cough may persist up to 3 weeks
- Hydration: Warm liquids (herbal teas, broths, warm water with honey and lemon)
- Rest: Adequate sleep and reduced activity
- Nasal saline irrigation for congestion relief
- Honey (age > 1 year): 1-2 teaspoons for cough relief (evidence-based)
- Steam inhalation with caution (NOT in children due to burn risk)

Sore Throat (Pharyngitis) Assessment:
- Most sore throats are viral (70-80%) and do NOT require antibiotics
- Centor Score for bacterial pharyngitis: Fever > 38°C, tonsillar exudates, tender anterior cervical lymph nodes, absence of cough
- Score 0-1: Low risk (1-10% streptococcal) → Supportive care only
- Score 4+: Higher risk (50-60% streptococcal) → Consider rapid strep test

When to Seek Medical Attention for URTI:
- Symptoms persisting > 10 days without improvement
- Fever > 39°C (102.2°F) lasting > 3 days
- Severe or worsening symptoms after initial improvement (secondary infection)
- Difficulty breathing or swallowing
- Stiff neck with fever
- Rash accompanying sore throat
- Immunocompromised individuals

Antibiotic Stewardship Note:
Antibiotics are NOT effective against viral infections. Inappropriate antibiotic use contributes to antimicrobial resistance, a critical global health threat. Demand for antibiotics should be addressed with patient education.

TITLE: Food Poisoning and Acute Gastroenteritis
SOURCE: WHO Food Safety Guidelines; CDC Foodborne Illness Management, 2023
CATEGORY: Gastroenterology | General Medicine

Common causes include Norovirus, Salmonella, E. coli, Campylobacter, and Staphylococcus aureus. Onset ranges from 1 hour (Staph aureus toxin) to 72 hours (Salmonella) after ingestion.

Symptom Assessment:
- Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea (watery or bloody)
- Abdominal cramps and pain
- Low-grade fever
- Headache, muscle aches, fatigue

Home Management (Mild Cases):
1. Oral Rehydration: Small, frequent sips of ORS, clear broths, diluted fruit juice
2. BRAT Diet when tolerated: Bananas, Rice, Applesauce, Toast
3. Avoid: Dairy, caffeine, alcohol, fatty, or spicy foods for 24-48 hours
4. Rest and gradual return to normal diet

Dehydration Assessment:
- Mild: Thirst, decreased urine output, slightly dry mouth
- Moderate: Very dry mouth, reduced skin turgor, sunken eyes, infrequent urination
- Severe: Extreme thirst, very sunken eyes, rapid heartbeat, lethargy, no urination for 8+ hours (EMERGENCY)

Red Flags Requiring Emergency Care:
- Bloody diarrhea or vomit
- Fever > 38.5°C (101.3°F) lasting > 24 hours
- Inability to keep any fluids down for > 12 hours
- Signs of severe dehydration
- Symptoms in infants, elderly, pregnant women, or immunocompromised
- Onset after consuming mushrooms, shellfish, or canned food (botulism risk)
